\section{Data representation and scaling}

In this section, the data representation choices used throughout the hardware architecture are discussed and motivated.
Because the proposed carrier recovery system is implemented entirely in digital hardware, the numerical representation
of both the received samples and the phase-rotation coefficients has a direct impact on the achievable accuracy, hardware
complexity and resource utilization.

\subsubsection{sample representation}

First, the representation of the received samples $r$ is discussed. Afterwards, the representation of the phase-rotation
coefficients used throughout the \ac{SBPS} and phase compensation stages is explained.

The received symbols are represented in Cartesian form as complex \ac{I/Q} samples:

\begin{equation}
r = I + jQ
\end{equation}

where both $I$ and $Q$ are stored using signed fixed-point formats. Cartesian \ac{I/Q} representation is preferred
over magnitude-phase representation because the majority of the DSP operations in the architecture consist of additions,
multiplications and comparisons on the real and imaginary components. These operations map efficiently onto FPGA DSP slices
and LUT-based arithmetic structures.

Furthermore, phase rotations in coherent DSP are naturally implemented using complex multiplication:

\begin{equation}
r' = r \cdot e^{-j\phi}
\end{equation}

Expanding this into Cartesian form yields:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cartesian-rotation}
I' = I\cos(\phi) + Q\sin(\phi), \qquad
Q' = -I\sin(\phi) + Q\cos(\phi)
\end{equation}

As a result, the phase-rotation coefficients are represented directly as fixed-point cosine and sine values rather than as
angles themselves. While storing the phase as an angle would simplify interpretation, it would require costly trigonometric
operations whenever a rotation has to be applied. By representing the phase coefficients directly as
$\cos(\phi)$ and $\sin(\phi)$, the hardware implementation only requires multiplications and additions during rotation.

The received symbols correspond to a normalized 16QAM constellation. For a square 16QAM constellation, the possible in-phase
and quadrature amplitudes are:

\begin{equation}
\pm s \qquad \pm 3s
\end{equation}

resulting in constellation points formed by all possible $(I,Q)$ combinations of these values.

To normalize the average constellation power to unity, the scaling factor $s$ is chosen as:

\begin{equation}
s = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}
\end{equation}

This normalization ensures that the average symbol energy equals one:

\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[|r|^2] = 1
\end{equation}

which is convenient for both simulation and hardware verification, since impairments such as \ac{SNR}, linewidth and residual
frequency offset can then be expressed independently of the constellation scaling.

\subsubsection{sample scaling}
Because FPGA resources and datapaths are limited, all samples are represented using fixed-point formats. Floating-point arithmetic would significantly increase the hardware complexity, routing overhead and power consumption
of the design. Fixed-point arithmetic, on the other hand, allows efficient mapping onto FPGA DSP resources while still providing
sufficient numerical precision for the considered coherent optical communication scenarios.

In this work, the received \ac{I/Q} samples are represented using signed Q1.15 fixed-point format. This corresponds to a
16-bit signed integer representation with one sign/integer bit and 15 fractional bits. The representable range is therefore:

\begin{equation}
-1 \leq x < 1
\end{equation}

Before mapping the constellation onto this range, the maximum possible signal excursion under phase rotation must first be considered.

The outer-most 16QAM constellation points are located at:

\begin{equation}
(\pm 3s,\pm 3s)
\end{equation}

These points have the largest magnitude:

\begin{equation}
|r_{\text{max}}| = \sqrt{(3s)^2 + (3s)^2}
= 3\sqrt{2}s
\end{equation}

Substituting $s = 1/\sqrt{10}$ yields:

\begin{equation}
|r_{\text{max}}| \approx 1.34
\end{equation}

This means that a pure phase rotation of the outer-most constellation points can already produce instantaneous
$I$ or $Q$ values larger than unity when the constellation aligns with one of the axes.

If this effect were not taken into account, clipping would occur during phase rotation. Such clipping is especially problematic
for the \ac{SBPS} metric calculations, since the Euclidean-distance calculations would no longer correctly represent the actual
distance to the ideal constellation points. As a result, false local minima could appear in the error function, potentially causing
incorrect phase estimation (as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:clipping_false_minima}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/clipping_false_minima.png}
\caption{Clipping causing false minima for SBPS metric}
\label{fig:clipping_false_minima}
\end{figure}

Besides phase rotation, \ac{AWGN} can further increase the instantaneous sample magnitude.
Designing the fixed-point range only for ideal phase-rotated constellation points would therefore still result in clipping
under low-\ac{SNR} conditions.

Ideally, the constellation scaling would adapt dynamically to the received signal quality such that the available integer range
is optimally utilized for every \ac{SNR}. However, implementing such adaptive scaling falls outside the scope of this thesis.
Instead, a fixed scaling factor is used throughout the architecture.

Taking into account both the maximum phase-rotated constellation magnitude and additional noise excursions, a static scaling factor
of

\begin{equation}
\alpha = 0.678
\end{equation}

is selected.

Using this scaling factor, the normalized constellation points are mapped onto the Q1.15 integer range as:

\begin{equation}
\pm 7025 \qquad \pm 3 \cdot 7025
\end{equation}

This scaling provides sufficient headroom for phase rotations and moderate noise excursions while still maintaining adequate
fixed-point precision for the DSP 16-bit operations.

Figure~\ref{fig:clipping_constellation} illustrates the effect of different \ac{SNR} conditions on the fixed-point representation, including the scaling factor $\alpha$.
At sufficiently low \ac{SNR}, the noisy constellation points exceed the representable range and clipping occurs. This clipping
degrades the accuracy of both the \ac{SBPS} metric calculations and the spread estimation calculations, ultimately reducing the
phase recovery performance of the architecture.

\caption{Clipping of 16QAM symbols under different SNR conditions}
\label{fig:clipping_constellation}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Phase representation}

The phase-related quantities used throughout the architecture are represented separately from the received \ac{I/Q} samples. 
Depending on the type of operation being performed, two different phase representations are used throughout the pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item trigonometric representation using $\cos(\phi)$ and $\sin(\phi)$
    \item direct phase-word representation using an angle $\phi$
\end{enumerate}

Both representations have advantages and disadvantages in terms of hardware complexity and ease of manipulation.

The trigonometric representation is inherently well-suited for applying phase rotations to complex samples. 
A rotation over phase $\phi$ can directly be implemented as seen in equation~\ref{eq:cartesian-rotation},
meaning that only multiplications and additions are required. This maps efficiently onto FPGA DSP slices and avoids the need 
for expensive real-time trigonometric calculations.

For this reason, the \ac{SBPS} stages internally use phase coefficients represented directly as fixed-point sine and cosine values. 
Since both $\sin(\phi)$ and $\cos(\phi)$ are bounded between $-1$ and $1$, these coefficients are represented using signed Q1.15 
fixed-point format.

However, while trigonometric representation is efficient for applying rotations, it is considerably less convenient for manipulating 
the phase itself. Operations such as phase accumulation or phase interpolation become significantly more complex when the phase is stored 
as sine and cosine coefficients.

For example, adding two phase terms $\phi_e$ and $\theta$ in trigonometric form requires the angle-sum identities:

\begin{align}
    \cos(\phi_e + \theta)
    &= \cos(\phi_e)\cos(\theta) - \sin(\phi_e)\sin(\theta)
    \\
    \sin(\phi_e + \theta)
    &= \sin(\phi_e)\cos(\theta) + \cos(\phi_e)\sin(\theta)
\end{align}

Rather than requiring a single addition, this operation now requires four multiplications and multiple additions. 
In contrast, when the phase is represented directly as a phase word, the same operation simply becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\text{new}} = \phi_e + \theta
\end{equation}

which only requires an adder.

Within the \ac{SBPS} module, this limitation is not problematic. The iterative test angles are static during runtime and are fully 
known at synthesis time. Therefore, all required sine and cosine coefficients can be precomputed and stored in small lookup tables. 
The different stage rotations, such as $\pm \pi/8$, $\pm \pi/16$ and subsequent refinements, are thus directly available in 
trigonometric form without requiring any runtime phase calculations.

The later pipeline stages, however, rely heavily on direct phase manipulation. The phase unwrapper calculates leading and lagging 
phase estimates, while the phase compensation stage interpolates phase values across the different sections of a block. These operations 
all naturally operate on phase words rather than trigonometric coefficients.

Furthermore, the \ac{CORDIC} IP used in the phase compensation stage also accepts phase-word inputs directly. This makes phase-word 
representation considerably more efficient for the downstream processing stages.

The spread estimation (\ac{SE}) module also inherently produces a phase-word representation. After the fourth-power operation removes 
the modulation, the phase spread is estimated using a normalized dot product (equation~\ref{eq:spread-cos}) 
followed by an arccosine operation (equation~\ref{eq:spread-mag}).

The output of this operation is fundamentally an angle estimate represented as a phase word. Representing this entire operation directly 
in trigonometric form would require repeated conversions between angular and Cartesian domains, significantly increasing the arithmetic complexity.

Taking into account the complete pipeline structure, a mixed representation strategy is therefore adopted. The \ac{SBPS} stages use 
trigonometric phase representation because their primary purpose is efficient sample rotation using static test angles. The downstream 
pipeline stages, however, use direct phase-word representation because they require frequent phase manipulations and interpolations.

To bridge both domains, the final \ac{SBPS} output coefficients are converted from trigonometric form into a phase word using a 
\ac{CORDIC}-based vectoring operation.

%     \phi = \arctan\left(\frac{Q}{I}\right)
% \end{equation}

\section{Serialized binary phase search block}

The hardware implementation follows a staged refinement architecture, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:functional-SBPS}. The same received block
\[
\bar r = \left(r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_{\mathcal W-1}\right)
\]
is presented to every stage, while only the accumulated phase estimate is updated from stage to stage. 
At stage \(m\), two candidate phase updates are evaluated around the previously selected estimate 
\(\phi_{m-1}\), namely \(\phi_{m-1}-\phi_t\) and \(\phi_{m-1}+\phi_t\). The candidate that produces 
the smaller block error is selected as the stage output \(\phi_m\), which is then forwarded to the next stage. 
By cascading several stages with progressively smaller test angles, the architecture converges towards the phase 
estimate associated with the centre of the block.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/SBPS_functional.png}
\caption{Functional diagram of the full SBPS architecture }
\label{fig:functional-SBPS}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Functional operation}

Figure~\ref{fig:functional-SBPS-stage} shows the internal organisation of a single stage. The upper branch corresponds to the clockwise (CW) update and evaluates \(\phi_{m-1}-\phi_t\), whereas the lower branch corresponds to the counter-clockwise (CCW) update and evaluates \(\phi_{m-1}+\phi_t\). Each branch contains a \textit{rotate metric slice} unit that processes the complete input block using its own candidate phase. The resulting block metrics are compared by the \textit{min metric decider}, and the associated candidate phase is selected by the final \ac{MUX}. In this way, each stage performs a binary phase refinement around the current estimate.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/SBPS_stage_functional.png}
\caption{Functional diagram of the SBPS stage}
\label{fig:functional-SBPS-stage}
\end{figure}

The \textit{rotate metric slice} unit is the core computational block of the design and is detailed in Fig.~\ref{fig:functional-rotate-slice-metric}. For every symbol in the block, the candidate phase is used to rotate the received sample, the rotated sample is sliced to a reference constellation point, and the squared Euclidean distance between the rotated and sliced values is computed. In the normal stages this reference is the nearest 16QAM point. In the first radius-directed stages, the slicer uses the modified decision rule discussed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:sbps-pattern-effect}. The resulting per-symbol errors are accumulated over the full block window to obtain a single decision metric for the branch. All symbols in the block are therefore processed in parallel inside each branch, while the serial refinement occurs across the SBPS stages.

In the implemented datapath, phase is not represented as an explicit angle word. Instead, each phase value is carried by its trigonometric components \((\cos(\phi), \sin(\phi))\) in fixed-point format. This allows the candidate phases to be generated directly by the \textit{angle add} block from the previous estimate \((\cos(\phi_{m-1}), \sin(\phi_{m-1}))\) and the stage test angle \((\cos(\phi_t), \sin(\phi_t))\):
\begin{align}
\cos(\phi_{m-1} \pm \phi_t)
&= \cos(\phi_{m-1})\cos(\phi_t) \mp \sin(\phi_{m-1})\sin(\phi_t), \\
\sin(\phi_{m-1} \pm \phi_t)
&= \sin(\phi_{m-1})\cos(\phi_t) \pm \cos(\phi_{m-1})\sin(\phi_t).
\end{align}

Once a candidate phase has been formed, the received samples are rotated in the \(I/Q\) plane. For a candidate angle
\[
\theta \in \left\{\phi_{m-1}-\phi_t,\; \phi_{m-1}+\phi_t\right\},
\]
the rotator computes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rotated_samples}
\begin{aligned}
I_k' &= I_k \cos(\theta) + Q_k \sin(\theta), \\
Q_k' &= -I_k \sin(\theta) + Q_k \cos(\theta),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
for every symbol \(r_k = I_k + jQ_k\) in the block. The rotated symbol is then denoted by \(r_k' = I_k' + jQ_k'\).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/rotate_slice_metric_data.png}
\caption{Datapath diagram of the \textit{rotate metric slice} unit }
\label{fig:data-rotate-slice-metric}
\end{figure}

For standard 16QAM operation, the slicer maps each rotated sample to the nearest reference point \(D[r_k']\). The axis decisions are derived from the conventional inner--outer thresholds, with \(\pm 2s\) defining the transition between the inner and outer levels of the constellation. The resulting single-symbol error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:single-symbol-error}
e_k = \left|r_k' - D[r_k']\right|^2
    = \left(I_k' - D_I[r_k']\right)^2
    + \left(Q_k' - D_Q[r_k']\right)^2,
\end{equation}
and the branch metric is obtained by accumulation over the complete block:
\begin{equation}
E = \sum_{k=0}^{\mathcal W-1} e_k.
\end{equation}
This accumulated metric is the quantity used by the \textit{min metric decider} to select the surviving branch.

\subsection{Radius-directed stages}

The motivation for the radius-directed stages is the pattern effect described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:sbps-pattern-effect}. During the first \ac{SBPS} stages the candidate phases are still separated by relatively large angles. In this regime, middle-ring 16QAM symbols can produce misleading nearest-neighbour decisions and may create false minima in the accumulated branch metric. The hardware therefore supports a modified slicer mode for the first \(G_{\mathrm{RAD\_DIRECTED\_N}}\) stages of the chain.

In the normal 16QAM slicer, each axis is decided independently using the threshold \(\pm 2s\):
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{16QAM}}(I',Q')
\in
\{\pm s,\pm 3s\}
\times
\{\pm s,\pm 3s\}.
\end{equation}
This implements the usual nearest-neighbour decision regions. In the radius-directed mode, an additional comparator threshold \(l/2\) is evaluated on the absolute values of the rotated components. If either component lies outside this central box, the symbol is forced to the corresponding outer corner:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{RD}}(I',Q')
=
\begin{cases}
\left(3s\,\mathrm{sgn}(I'),\,3s\,\mathrm{sgn}(Q')\right),
& |I'| \geq l/2 \ \lor\ |Q'| \geq l/2,\\
D_{\mathrm{16QAM}}(I',Q'),
& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:radius-directed-slicer}
\end{equation}

With \(l\) chosen such that the central box covers the inner constellation points but not the high-amplitude middle/outer points, this rule removes explicit middle-ring decisions from the coarse stages. The metric is then dominated by the inner and corner-like symbol classes, which have a more reliable angular relation to the carrier phase. This is cheaper than computing the Euclidean radius of every rotated sample, since the implementation only needs absolute-value operations and two comparisons per symbol.

The generic \(G_{\mathrm{RAD\_DIRECTED\_N}}\) controls how many early stages use this slicer mode. For stage index \(k\), the generated chain sets the stage to radius-directed operation when \(k < G_{\mathrm{RAD\_DIRECTED\_N}}\). Once the phase estimate has been sufficiently refined, later stages revert to the conventional 16QAM slicer. This keeps the robustness benefit during coarse acquisition while preserving the final-stage resolution of the full 16QAM decision metric.

\subsection{Input selector}
\subsubsection{Functional operation}

As introduced in Section~\ref{input selector}, the spread and direction estimation blocks require a set of representative symbols from the 
input block. However, not all symbols are suitable for this purpose. Therefore, an input selection stage is required to extract valid 
candidate symbols from different regions of the block.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/SE_input_logic_functional.png}
\caption{Functional diagram of the SE input selector}
\label{fig:SE_input_selector_functional}
\end{figure}

The input selector operates on the full block of received symbols $ \bar{r} $, and outputs three candidate symbols $x$, $y$, and $z$, 
corresponding to the first, middle, and last regions of the block, respectively. These candidates are subsequently used for spread and
direction estimation.

The selection process consists of three sequential steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Identification of valid candidate symbols within each region of the block.
    \item Validation of the combination of candidates across regions.
    \item Latching or updating of the selected symbols for further processing.
\end{enumerate}

These steps are implemented by the \textit{n-type slicer}, \textit{cands\_valid\_combo}, and \textit{cand\_symbol\_latch} modules, respectively.

Each region (first, middle, last) is processed by an independent \textit{n-type slicer}. 
This module evaluates a small subset of symbols (three in this implementation) and determines 
whether a suitable candidate is present. A symbol is considered valid if it lies on the inner or 
outer ring of the constellation, which ensures reliable spread estimation (this criterion is further 
elaborated in the algorithmic description of the spread estimator). The index of the first valid symbol 
is returned via the \textit{cand\_idx} bus. If no valid candidate is found, the \textit{cands\_any} flag is deasserted.

The \textit{cands\_valid\_combo} module evaluates the availability of candidates across the three regions.
A valid combination is only accepted if at least two regions provide a candidate symbol. If this condition is not met,
 all candidate flags are suppressed. This constraint reduces the likelihood of unreliable spread or direction estimates, 
 which would otherwise arise from insufficient spatial information within the block.

Finally, the \textit{cand\_symbol\_latch} module selects and outputs the candidate symbols. For each region where the 
\textit{cands\_any} flag is asserted, the corresponding symbol is extracted from the input block using the provided 
\textit{cand\_idx}. If valid candidates are present, the outputs are updated and the \textit{update} signal is asserted. 
Otherwise, the previous outputs are retained and the \textit{hold} signal is asserted. In this way, consistency is maintained 
when no valid candidate combination is available.

\subsubsection{Timing flow}

The timing behaviour of the input selector is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:SE_input_selector_timing}.

The \textit{n-type slicer} requires two clock cycles to determine and register a valid candidate index. The subsequent 
\textit{cands\_valid\_combo} stage introduces an additional cycle to produce stable validity signals.

To ensure correct operation of the \textit{cand\_symbol\_latch}, all inputs must be temporally aligned. In particular, 
the candidate indices and their corresponding validity flags must be synchronized with the original input symbols. To achieve this, 
delay elements are inserted in the data path. The input symbol block $ \bar{r} $ is delayed by three clock cycles, matching the 
combined latency of the slicer and combination stages. The \textit{cand\_idx} signals are delayed by one cycle to align with the 
output of the \textit{cands\_valid\_combo} module.

\centering

\caption{Timing flow diagram of the SE input selector}
\label{fig:SE_input_selector_timing}
\end{figure}

The \textit{cand\_symbol\_latch} itself introduces one additional clock cycle to register the selected symbols. 
As a result, the total latency of the input selector amounts to four clock cycles.

\subsection{Fourth-power I/Q calculators}

\subsubsection{Functional operation}

The fourth-power calculators are an important part of the spread estimation algorithm. 
The purpose of the fourth-power operation is to suppress the rotational symmetry of the modulation and 
to make the carrier-induced phase evolution more directly observable.

For a complex input sample
\begin{equation}
    r = I + jQ ,
\end{equation}
the block calculates
\begin{equation}
    r^4 = (I+jQ)^4 .
\end{equation}
The output is again represented as a complex value, with a real and imaginary component. 
This operation is multiplication-heavy compared to most other operations in the low-complexity 
carrier recovery system. This is relevant because fourth-power based frequency or spread 
estimation is one of the computationally dominant operations in such systems: earlier work on 
this carrier recovery approach explicitly identifies the complex fourth-power operation as a 
large computation, requiring several real-valued multiplications per symbol 
\cite{Jonas}.
\\\\
In this implementation, the input samples are provided as signed fixed-point values in Q1.15 
format. The fourth-power output is not rescaled back to Q1.15, but is kept at a much larger 
internal word length. This avoids unnecessary loss of precision in the following dot-product, 
sign and angle-estimation calculations without significantly effecting the compute needed for those 
calculations.

\subsubsection{Data flow}

Although the mathematical expression $r^4$ can be expanded directly, the hardware 
implementation avoids calculating all fourth-order terms independently. Instead, the 
calculation is structured as two consecutive squaring operations:
$$
    r^2 = (I+jQ)^2 = (I^2-Q^2) + j(2IQ).
$$
Defining
$$
    a = I^2 - Q^2,
    \qquad
    b = 2IQ,
$$
the fourth power becomes
$$
    r^4 = (r^2)^2 = (a+jb)^2 .
$$

This gives
\begin{equation}
    r^4 = (a^2-b^2) + j(2ab).
\end{equation}

The real and imaginary fourth-power outputs are therefore calculated as
\begin{align}\label{eq:re_and_im_fourth_power}
    \Re\{r^4\} &= a^2 - b^2, \\
    \Im\{r^4\} &= 2ab.
\end{align}

This decomposition maps naturally onto a pipelined multiplier structure. The first stage 
calculates the three second-order products $I^2$, $Q^2$ and $IQ$. From these values, the 
second stage forms the intermediate real and imaginary components of $r^2$: $a=I^2-Q^2$ 
and $b=2IQ$. The multiplication by two is implemented as a left shift, so no additional 
multiplier is required. The third stage then calculates $a^2$, $b^2$ and $ab$. Finally, the 
fourth stage combines these terms into the real and imaginary components of the fourth-power 
output.

The resulting implementation requires six real multiplications: three multiplications in the 
first stage and three multiplications in the third stage. The remaining operations are 
additions, subtractions and shifts. This is more efficient than a direct polynomial expansion 
of $(I+jQ)^4$, because the intermediate square $r^2$ is reused.

The bit growth is also handled explicitly. Since the input samples are signed values with 
width $W$, the products $I^2$, $Q^2$ and $IQ$ require approximately $2W$ bits. The 
intermediate values $a$ and $b$ require one additional bit, because $a$ is formed by a 
subtraction and $b$ by a shift-left operation. Squaring $a$ and $b$ then doubles this width 
again. For this reason, the output width is chosen conservatively as
\begin{equation}
    W_{\mathrm{out}} \geq 4W + 3 .
\end{equation}
For the default input width of $W=16$, this gives a required full-precision output width of 
at least 67 bits. The implementation uses a default output width of 70 bits, which provides 
additional margin and avoids overflow during the fourth-power calculation.

The use of a large fourth-power output width is acceptable here because the block is only 
applied to selected candidate symbols in the spread estimation path, rather than to every 
symbol in every blind phase search test angle. This fits the broader objective of the proposed 
architecture: reducing the number of expensive carrier recovery operations while preserving 
enough phase information to improve tracking of residual frequency offset.

\subsubsection{Timing flow}

The fourth-power calculator is implemented as a four-stage registered pipeline. Figure~\ref{fig:SE-fourth-power} shows the calculation flow through these stages.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/SE_fourth_power_calculations.png}
\caption{Calculation-flow of the fourth-power module}
\label{fig:SE-fourth-power}
\end{figure}

Each block in the figure corresponds to one clocked pipeline stage. The first stage performs 
the initial second-order products and registers the results internally. These intermediate 
values are then forwarded to the second stage, where the intermediate complex representation of 
the squared sample is formed.

The third stage contains the second multiplication layer of the architecture. Here, the 
intermediate terms generated in the previous stage are squared and multiplied again in 
preparation for the final fourth-power reconstruction. In the fourth and final stage, the 
real and imaginary outputs are assembled and driven to the output ports.

A valid signal is propagated alongside the datapath through the internal pipeline control signals 
\texttt{s1\_valid}, \texttt{s2\_valid}, \texttt{s3\_valid} and \texttt{s4\_valid}. This ensures that 
the output validity remains aligned with the corresponding fourth-power result throughout the pipeline.

The total latency of the module is therefore four clock cycles from assertion of \texttt{in\_valid} 
to assertion of \texttt{out\_valid}.

\subsubsection{CORDIC-based alternative}

A possible alternative implementation would be to use a CORDIC-based polar approach. In such 
a design, the input sample would first be converted from Cartesian form to magnitude and 
phase:
\begin{equation}
    r = |r|e^{j\phi}.
\end{equation}
The fourth power could then be calculated as
\begin{equation}
    r^4 = |r|^4 e^{j4\phi}.
\end{equation}
Conceptually, this is attractive because the phase multiplication by four is simple, and the 
CORDIC algorithm is well suited for angle extraction and vector rotation.

However, this approach is not necessarily beneficial in the context of this low-complexity 
architecture. A CORDIC implementation would require an angle extraction step, additional 
handling of the magnitude term, and a rotation or sine/cosine reconstruction step. This 
introduces iterative logic, latency and control complexity. The fourth-power block used here, 
by contrast, is a feed-forward arithmetic pipeline consisting only of multipliers, adders, 
subtractors and shifts. It also produces exactly the Cartesian components required by the 
following dot-product and sign-estimation logic.

The main advantage of a CORDIC-based approach would be multiplier reduction, especially in 
an FPGA design where DSP slices are scarce or already heavily used. Its main disadvantage is 
that the algorithm becomes less direct: the system would temporarily move to a polar 
representation, only to return to Cartesian components for the following processing stages. 
For this design, the multiplier-based fourth-power calculator is therefore the considered implementation. It 
keeps the data path simple, deterministic and easy to pipeline, while avoiding the additional 
latency and format conversions of a CORDIC-based solution.

\subsection{Spread estimation}
\label{subsec:spread-estimation}

This subsection describes the part of the spread-estimation backend that calculates the absolute 
phase-spread estimate. The focus here is the computation from a pair of selected complex samples to the unsigned angle estimate \( |\theta| \).
The top overview is given in the figure below:

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/SE_top.png}
\caption{Top overview of the spread estimation module}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Functional operation}

After the selected samples have been raised to the fourth power, the spread-estimation block calculates the angle between the two resulting complex vectors. Let
\[
    r_0^{(4)} = I_0^{(4)} + jQ_0^{(4)}, \qquad
    r_1^{(4)} = I_1^{(4)} + jQ_1^{(4)} .
\]
where $ I_x^{(4)} $ and $ Q_x^{(4)} $ are the real and imaginary part respectively as also seen from equation~\ref{eq:re_and_im_fourth_power}.
The numerator of the cosine estimate is the dot product of these two fourth-power vectors:
\[
    \left\langle r_0^{(4)}, r_1^{(4)} \right\rangle  = I_0^{(4)} I_1^{(4)} + Q_0^{(4)} Q_1^{(4)} .
\]
This dot product is proportional to
\[
    |r_0^{(4)}|\,|r_1^{(4)}|\cos(4|\theta|),
\]
so the required normalization factor is the reciprocal of the product of the two fourth-power vector magnitudes.

For the 16-QAM constellation used here, the denominator is not computed with a run-time divider. Instead, each sample is sliced as belonging to the inner or outer diagonal radius of the constellation. The two inner/outer flags address a small radius-product LUT. This \textit{radius\_prod\_LUT} directly returns a precomputed reciprocal multiplication factor for the possible radius products:
\[
    R^{-1}_{\mathrm{II}}, \qquad
    R^{-1}_{\mathrm{IO}}, \qquad
    R^{-1}_{\mathrm{OO}} .
\]
The reciprocal factors are scaled fixed-point constants, so the normalization is implemented as one signed multiplication followed by an arithmetic right shift by the \textit{signed\_unit\_recip\_mult} block. The result is saturated to the interval representable by Q1.31 and represents
\[
    x = \cos(4|\theta|).
\]

The final conversion from \(x\) to an angle is performed by an arccosine LUT. Since the desired output is
\[
    |\theta| = \frac{1}{4}\arccos(x),
\]
the LUT stores \(\arccos(x)/4\) rather than \(\arccos(x)\). Only the half-domain \(x \in [0,1]\) is stored, as marked in Figure~\ref{fig:LUT}. For negative inputs, the symmetry
\[
    \frac{\arccos(-x)}{4}
    = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\arccos(x)}{4}
\]
is used to reconstruct the correct value. The output of this stage is therefore the absolute phase-spread estimate \( |\theta| \), limited to the range \(0 \leq |\theta| \leq \pi/4\).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/LUT.png}
\caption{\(\arccos(x)/4\) LUT symmetry}
\label{fig:LUT}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection{Data flow}

The spread-estimation calculation is implemented as a fixed-point pipeline. The selected input samples are signed \(G_{\mathrm{sample}}\)-bit values. In the implemented configuration, \(G_{\mathrm{sample}} = 16\), so these are 16-bit signed I/Q samples. The fourth-power stage produces signed real and imaginary components with width \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}}\). As discussed in the previous subsection, the implemented configuration uses \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}} = 70\). These components are kept at this extended precision because the subsequent dot product multiplies two fourth-power values.

The dot-product output has width \(2G_{\mathrm{pow4}}+1\) and is signed, since the angle between the two fourth-power vectors can produce a negative cosine. For the implemented default \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}} = 70\), this gives a 141-bit signed dot-product result. In parallel, the delayed original I/Q samples are converted into inner/outer radius flags. The radius-product LUT outputs a signed 48-bit reciprocal factor. The constants are scaled such that multiplying the dot product by the LUT value and shifting right by \(G_{\mathrm{inv\_frac}}\) fractional bits produces a normalized Q1.31 value. Unlike \(G_{\mathrm{sample}}\) and \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}}\), the value of \(G_{\mathrm{inv\_frac}}\) is tied to the chosen reciprocal-constant scaling and is therefore kept as a symbolic implementation parameter in this description.

The normalized value is interpreted as a signed Q1.31 representation of \( \cos(4|\theta|) \), which corresponds to a 32-bit signed fixed-point word. Before addressing the arccosine LUT, the absolute value is taken. The special case \(x=-1\) is saturated to the largest positive Q1.31 value before taking the absolute value, avoiding overflow in two's-complement representation. The LUT address is formed from the upper fractional bits of \(|x|\), specifically bits 30 down to 19, giving a 12-bit address and therefore 4096 table entries.

The arccosine LUT output is also Q1.31. It stores \(\arccos(|x|)/4\) for non-negative input magnitudes. A delayed sign bit from the original Q1.31 cosine value selects either the direct LUT output or the reconstructed value \(\pi/4-\mathrm{LUT}(|x|)\). Thus the final output is a signed Q1.31 number, but it represents the non-negative angle \( |\theta| \).

\subsubsection{Timing flow}

The block is fully pipelined. Once the pipeline is filled, a new valid sample pair can be accepted every clock cycle, provided both input-valid signals are asserted. The latency from the selected input pair to the \( |\theta| \) output is eight clock cycles in the current implementation.

Because the architecture contains two parallel branches, a single cumulative-latency column can be misleading. Table~\ref{tab:spread-estimation-latency} therefore reports the cumulative latency separately for each path up to the alignment point. After both branches have been aligned, the remaining stages are shown as a common path. In the table, ``Cum.'' is path-local before the merge and is measured from the original input pair after the merge.

The fourth-power dot-product path and the denominator-reciprocal path are explicitly balanced. The denominator path starts with a three-clock delay of the original I/Q samples so that its reciprocal factor becomes valid in the same clock cycle as the dot product. Both branches therefore align after five clock cycles. The reciprocal multiplication stage only asserts its output-valid signal when both the dot product and the reciprocal factor are valid. The arccosine stage then adds two clocks, matching the LUT read latency and the delayed sign/valid signals used for half-domain reconstruction.

\subsection{Sign estimation}
\label{subsec:sign-estimation}

The absolute spread-estimation path determines the magnitude \( |\theta| \). A separate sign-estimation path determines whether this magnitude must be applied as a positive or negative phase estimate. This subsection describes the blocks that generate this final sign decision and align it with the absolute-angle output.
An overview of this topology can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:Sign-top}.

\subsubsection{Functional operation}

The sign estimator uses the orientation of two fourth-power complex vectors. For two selected fourth-power samples
\[
    r_0^{(4)} = I_0^{(4)} + jQ_0^{(4)}, \qquad
    r_1^{(4)} = I_1^{(4)} + jQ_1^{(4)},
\]
the sign is determined from the two-dimensional cross product
\[
    r_0^{(4)} \times r_1^{(4)} = I_0^{(4)}Q_1^{(4)} - Q_0^{(4)}I_1^{(4)} .
\]
This quantity is proportional to
\[
    |r_0^{(4)}|\,|r_1^{(4)}|\sin(\phi_1-\phi_0),
\]
so its sign gives the rotation direction between the two fourth-power vectors. A positive cross product corresponds to one rotation direction, while a negative cross product corresponds to the opposite direction. Only the sign of $ r_0^{(4)} \times r_1^{(4)}$ is needed; the magnitude of the cross product is discarded.

The sign path receives three candidate symbols: first, middle and last. The sign-pair decision block selects which adjacent pair should be used for the sign estimate. If the first and middle candidates are both marked as updated, the sign is calculated from the first--middle pair. Otherwise, if the middle and last candidates are both marked as updated, the sign is calculated from the middle--last pair. If neither update combination is present, the previously selected pair is held.

Once the pair has been selected, the cross-product sign block computes the two products \(I_0^{(4)}Q_1^{(4)}\) and \(Q_0^{(4)}I_1^{(4)}\), subtracts them, and encodes the sign as a two-bit value. In the final sign-application block, the code \texttt{"11"} is interpreted as a positive sign and \texttt{"01"} as a negative sign. The signed angle is then generated as
\[
    \theta =
    \begin{cases}
        -|\theta|, & \text{if the sign code is \texttt{"01"},}\\
        \phantom{-}|\theta|, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\]

When the optional sign filter is enabled, the raw sign decisions are passed through a rolling majority filter. This filter stores the last \(2^{G_{\mathrm{log2\_signwin}}}\) sign decisions and outputs the majority sign. In case of an exact tie, the previous output sign is retained. This avoids rapid sign toggling when consecutive estimates are close to the decision boundary.

\subsubsection{Data flow}

The sign estimator operates on the same fourth-power representation used by the absolute-angle calculation. The first and last fourth-power values are exposed by the absolute spread-estimation backend, while the middle symbol is passed through an additional fourth-power block. Each fourth-power vector consists of signed real and imaginary components with width \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}}\). In the implemented configuration, \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}} = 70\), so both the real and imaginary fourth-power components are 70-bit signed values.

The update and hold control signals from the candidate-selection logic are Boolean control signals. Since the fourth-power pipeline has a latency of four clock cycles, these control signals are delayed by the same amount before they are used by the sign-pair decision block. This ensures that the delayed update decisions refer to the fourth-power samples currently present at the sign-pair selector inputs.

The selected sign pair consists of two signed complex vectors:
\[
    (I_0^{(4)}, Q_0^{(4)}) \quad \text{and} \quad
    (I_1^{(4)}, Q_1^{(4)}).
\]
The cross-product block multiplies two \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}}\)-bit signed values, producing two signed products of width \(2G_{\mathrm{pow4}}\). With \(G_{\mathrm{pow4}} = 70\), each product is therefore 140 bits wide. The subtraction is performed with one additional bit, giving a signed difference width of \(2G_{\mathrm{pow4}}+1\). In the implemented configuration this corresponds to a 141-bit signed difference. Only the sign of this difference is encoded at the output.

The raw sign output is a two-bit code. The final theta-signing block interprets \texttt{"01"} as a negative sign and negates the Q1.31 absolute-angle value in that case. All other sign codes produce a positive output. The resulting signed angle remains in the same fixed-point format as the absolute-angle input.

\subsubsection{Timing flow}

The sign path is timed to match the absolute-angle pipeline. The fourth-power outputs are available after four clock cycles. The update and hold control signals are therefore delayed by four clock cycles before pair selection. The pair selector registers the selected pair, the cross-product sign block uses two pipeline stages, and the sign output is delayed by one additional clock. This gives an eight-clock latency from the candidate-symbol inputs to the raw sign output, matching the eight-clock latency of the \( |\theta| \) path.

Here too, two branches exist before alignment: the fourth-power data path and the delayed update/hold control path. As above, ``Cum.'' is path-local before the merge and is measured from the original input pair after the merge.

\subsection{Filters}
\label{subsec:spread-estimation-filters}

Two optional filters are placed after the raw spread-estimation backend. The first filter smooths the absolute angle estimate, while the second filter smooths the sign decision. They are implemented separately because the two signals have different data types: the angle magnitude is a fixed-point numerical value, whereas the sign is a two-bit decision.

\subsubsection{Magnitude moving-average filter}

The magnitude filter is a rolling moving average over the Q-format angle estimate. Its window length is constrained to a power of two:
\[
    N_{\mathrm{avg}} = 2^{G_{\mathrm{log2\_avg}}}.
\]
This makes the final division by the window length a right shift instead of a general divider to reduce computational complexity. The filter stores the last \(N_{\mathrm{avg}}\) input samples in a circular buffer and maintains a running sum. For every new valid input sample \(x[n]\), the oldest sample in the buffer is subtracted and the new sample is added:
\[
    S[n] = S[n-1] + x[n] - x[n-N_{\mathrm{avg}}].
\]
The filtered output is then
\[
    y[n] = \frac{S[n]}{N_{\mathrm{avg}}},
\]
implemented as an arithmetic right shift by \(G_{\mathrm{log2\_avg}}\) bits.

The accumulator width is increased by \(G_{\mathrm{log2\_avg}}\) bits compared to the input width. This provides enough range to hold the sum of \(N_{\mathrm{avg}}\) signed input samples without overflow under normal fixed-point assumptions. If \(G_{\mathrm{log2\_avg}}=0\), the window length is one and the filter becomes a direct pass-through.

At start-up, the first valid sample is replicated across the entire logical window. This means the first filtered output is equal to the first input sample, instead of requiring a long fill period before a valid average can be produced. After this initialization, every valid input produces one valid filtered output.

\subsubsection{Sign rolling-majority filter}

The sign filter performs a rolling majority vote over the raw sign decisions, a graphical overview of its functional principles are given in Figure~\ref{fig:circular-buffer}. Its window length is also a power of two:
\[
    N_{\mathrm{sign}} = 2^{G_{\mathrm{log2\_signwin}}}.
\]
The incoming sign is encoded as a two-bit value, where \texttt{"11"} represents a positive sign and \texttt{"01"} represents a negative sign. Internally, the filter stores only one bit per sign decision: positive signs are stored as 1 and negative signs as 0.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/circular_buffer.png}
\caption{Sign rolling-majority filter with circular buffer}
\label{fig:circular-buffer}
\end{figure}

As with the moving-average filter, the sign filter uses a circular buffer. Instead of storing a numerical sum of signed values, it keeps a count of the number of positive signs currently present in the window. For every valid input sign, the oldest stored bit is removed from the count and the new sign bit is added. The output is then decided by comparing the positive count with half the window length:
\[
    \mathrm{sign}_{\mathrm{out}} =
    \begin{cases}
        +, & \text{if } N_+ > N_{\mathrm{sign}}/2,\\
        -, & \text{if } N_+ < N_{\mathrm{sign}}/2,\\
        \text{previous sign}, & \text{if } N_+ = N_{\mathrm{sign}}/2.
    \end{cases}
\]
The tie case is handled by holding the previous output sign. This prevents the filtered sign from toggling when the window contains an equal number of positive and negative decisions.

At reset, the sign-memory contents and output are initialized to the positive sign. On the first valid input, the whole logical window is initialized with that first sign, so the first output follows the first real sign decision. If \(G_{\mathrm{log2\_signwin}}=0\), the window length is one and the filter becomes a direct sign pass-through.



\subsubsection{Timing and alignment}

Both filters are streaming filters: after initialization, each valid input produces one valid output. The moving-average filter and the sign-majority filter each register their output, so they add one clock cycle of processing latency relative to their valid input. Because both filtered paths are driven from already aligned raw backend outputs, using the same valid-driven structure keeps the filtered magnitude and filtered sign aligned for the final signed-\(\theta\) generation.

\subsection{Magnitude and Sign integration}
\label{subsec:spread-estimation-magnitude-sign-integration}

After filtering, the angle estimate is reconstructed by combining the filtered magnitude with the filtered sign decision. The magnitude filter provides the absolute value \(\lvert \theta_{\mathrm{filt}} \rvert\), while the sign filter determines whether this magnitude should be interpreted as a positive or negative angle. The final signed estimate is therefore formed as
\[
    \theta_{\mathrm{out}} =
    \begin{cases}
        \phantom{-}\lvert \theta_{\mathrm{filt}} \rvert, & \text{if } \mathrm{sign}_{\mathrm{filt}} = +,\\
        -\lvert \theta_{\mathrm{filt}} \rvert, & \text{if } \mathrm{sign}_{\mathrm{filt}} = -.
    \end{cases}
\]

At last, the output valid signal is asserted only when both filtered components are valid, producing one aligned signed-\(\theta\) value for each accepted raw estimate.

\section{Phase unwrapper}
\label{sec:hardware-phase-unwrapper}

The phase unwrapper receives the wrapped centre-phase estimate from the \ac{SBPS} chain and the signed phase-spread estimate from the \ac{SE} block. In the implemented system this logic is part of the \textit{PUPC} block, but it is described separately here because it forms the stateful boundary between the blockwise estimator and the final phase compensator.
The top overview of the implementated hardware with annotated timing is illustrated below.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/PU_top.png}
\caption{Top overview of phase unwrapper block}
\label{fig:PU-top}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Functional operation}

The RTL hierarchy is split into two modules. The \textit{phase\_unwrapper\_top} module first reconstructs the leading and lagging edge phases of the current block, while the \textit{phase\_unwrapper} module performs the actual branch tracking. The edge reconstruction is implemented by the \textit{lead\_lag\_calculator}:
\begin{align}
    \phi_{\mathrm{lead}} &= \phi_e + \frac{\theta}{2},\\
    \phi_{\mathrm{lag}}  &= \phi_e - \frac{\theta}{2}.
\end{align}
Here, \(\phi_e\) is the wrapped centre phase in Q4.28 and \(\theta\) is the signed spread estimate in the same format. The division by two is implemented as an arithmetic right shift, so the Q-format is preserved.

For every valid block after the first one, the unwrapper compares the current lagging edge with the stored leading edge of the previous block:
\begin{equation}
    d_b = \phi_{\mathrm{lag},b} - \phi_{\mathrm{lead},b-1}.
\end{equation}
If this difference crosses the \(\pm \pi/4\) decision threshold, the two-bit unwrap counter is updated by one quadrant. A difference larger than or equal to \(+\pi/4\) decrements the counter, while a difference below \(-\pi/4\) increments it. The counter is stored modulo four, because the final constellation is unchanged by an additional \(2\pi\) rotation. The counter value selects one of four Q4.28 offsets:
\[
    0,\quad \frac{\pi}{2},\quad \pi,\quad \frac{3\pi}{2}.
\]
This offset is added to the delayed centre phase to produce the unwrapped centre phase. The first valid block is passed through without a correction, because no previous leading edge is available yet. The current leading edge is then stored for the next block.

\subsection{Data flow}

All phase quantities inside the unwrapper are signed 32-bit Q4.28 words. The constants \(\pi/4\), \(\pi/2\), \(\pi\), and \(3\pi/2\) are stored directly in the same representation. The branch state itself is only a two-bit unsigned value, exposed at the PUPC boundary as \textit{dbg\_pu\_idx} for simulation and debugging.

The \textit{phase\_unwrapper\_top} module delays the centre phase by one clock cycle so that it is aligned with the lead and lag outputs of the \textit{lead\_lag\_calculator}. The unwrapping decision therefore receives three aligned values: the current leading edge, the current lagging edge and the current centre phase. The output remains a Q4.28 centre-phase word, so no conversion is required before the section-coefficient generator.

\subsection{Timing flow}

The lead/lag calculation is registered and contributes one clock cycle. The centre phase is delayed by the same amount inside \textit{phase\_unwrapper\_top}. The \textit{phase\_unwrapper} then performs the edge-difference comparison, counter update, offset selection and output registration in the following cycle. The unwrapped phase is therefore available two clock cycles after a valid wrapped phase and spread estimate enter \textit{phase\_unwrapper\_top}.

The surrounding \textit{phase\_unwrap\_comp\_top} module delays \(\theta\) by the same two clock cycles before passing it to the section-phase generator. This keeps the spread estimate aligned with the unwrapped centre phase used for the final compensation coefficients.

\section{Phase compensation block}
\label{sec:hardware-phase-compensation}

The phase compensation block forms the final stage of the carrier-recovery datapath. It combines the unwrapped centre phase, the signed spread estimate and the delayed received symbol block to generate one CORDIC rotation phase per symbol. In the RTL this functionality is implemented by the \textit{PUPC} hierarchy. The top-level \textit{PUPC} module (see Figure~\ref{fig:PC-top}) first converts and aligns the incoming signals, after which the \textit{phase\_correction\_rotate\_top} module generates the per-symbol phases and rotates the complete block.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/PC_top.png}
\caption{Top overview of phase compensation block}
\label{fig:PC-top}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Functional operation}

The \ac{SBPS} chain represents its selected centre phase as Q1.15 cosine and sine values. Since the phase unwrapper and compensation logic operate on explicit phase words, the \textit{PUPC} top level converts these two trigonometric components to a signed Q4.28 angle using the \textit{trig\_2\_rad} module, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:PU-top}. The signed spread estimate \(\theta\) already arrives as Q4.28 and is therefore only delayed to match the \textit{trig\_2\_rad} latency. The input I/Q block is delayed by the same amount so that samples, centre phase and spread estimate enter the correction stage in the same clock cycle.

After phase unwrapping, the \textit{phase\_compensation\_coeffs} module generates one representative phase per compensation section. The section coefficients are computed at elaboration time from
\begin{equation}
    c_n =
    \frac{2n+1-N_{\mathrm{sec}}}{2N_{\mathrm{sec}}},
    \qquad n=0,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{sec}}-1,
\end{equation}
and stored as signed fixed-point constants with \(G_{\mathrm{COEFF\_FRAC}}\) fractional bits. For each valid block, every section phase is then computed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:comp_sections}
    \phi_n = \tilde{\phi}_e + c_n \theta .
\end{equation}
The multiplication \(c_n\theta\) is followed by an arithmetic right shift by \(G_{\mathrm{COEFF\_FRAC}}\) bits, returning the result to the Q4.28 phase format before it is added to the unwrapped centre phase.

The generated section phases are converted to the CORDIC phase format and expanded to per-symbol phases. The \textit{section\_phase\_expander} assumes that \(G_{\mathrm{NUM\_SYM}}\) is evenly divisible by \(G_{\mathrm{NSEC}}\), so each section contains
\[
    G_{\mathrm{SYM\_PER\_SEC}} =
    \frac{G_{\mathrm{NUM\_SYM}}}{G_{\mathrm{NSEC}}}
\]
symbols. For symbol index \(k\), the applied phase is selected from section \(k/G_{\mathrm{SYM\_PER\_SEC}}\). The resulting per-symbol phases drive a parallel \textit{cordic\_rotate\_bank}, which instantiates one Xilinx CORDIC rotator per symbol.

\subsection{Data flow}

The PUPC datapath uses three phase representations. The \ac{SBPS} output enters as Q1.15 sine and cosine. The internal unwrapping and section-generation path uses signed Q4.28 phase words. Finally, the phases passed to the CORDIC IP are converted to Q3.13. During this conversion, the Q4.28 section phase is first wrapped into the interval \([-\pi,\pi]\), then rounded by adding or subtracting \(2^{14}\), and finally shifted right by 15 bits.

The I/Q samples enter the block as separate packed Q1.15 buses. Before the CORDIC bank, the \textit{input\_reformatter} converts every symbol to the CORDIC Cartesian format by shifting Q1.15 to Q2.14. The per-symbol Cartesian word is packed as \(\{X,Y\}\). After rotation, the CORDIC outputs are interpreted as Q2.14 and shifted left by one bit to return to Q1.15. The output block is again presented as separate packed I and Q buses.

The implemented Vivado IP instance is parameterized with \(G_{\mathrm{NUM\_SYM}}=32\), \(G_{\mathrm{NSEC}}=16\), \(G_{\mathrm{SAMPLE\_WIDTH}}=16\), \(G_{\mathrm{PHASE\_WIDTH}}=32\), \(G_{\mathrm{COEFF\_WIDTH}}=16\), \(G_{\mathrm{COEFF\_FRAC}}=14\), \(G_{\mathrm{PHASE\_OUT\_WIDTH}}=16\), and \(G_{\mathrm{DELAY}}=18\). These generics keep the block-size, section count and fixed-point formats explicit, while allowing the same hierarchy to be regenerated for other sweep configurations.

\subsection{Timing flow}

At the PUPC top level, the atan2 conversion defines the initial alignment point. The RTL uses an 18-cycle delay for both the Q4.28 spread estimate and the packed I/Q symbol block, matching the atan2 output latency. The valid signal into the phase-correction backend is asserted only when the atan2 output, delayed theta and delayed symbol block are all valid.

Inside \textit{phase\_correction\_input\_top}, the phase unwrapping and section-phase generation require three clock cycles: two cycles for \textit{phase\_unwrapper\_top} and one cycle for \textit{phase\_compensation\_coeffs}. The I/Q block is delayed by three cycles in parallel. The \textit{input\_reformatter} then adds one registered conversion cycle, and the \textit{section\_phase\_expander} adds one further cycle. The Cartesian path is delayed by one cycle after reformatting so that it remains aligned with the expanded phase vector.

\section{Top-design overview}

The previous sections described the main processing blocks individually. The top design combines these blocks into a single blockwise carrier-recovery datapath. Its purpose is not to introduce a new estimation algorithm, but to connect the already discussed \ac{SBPS}, \ac{SE}, phase unwrapping and phase-compensation blocks while preserving the correct timing relation between their outputs.

The implemented wrapper is built around one received block interface. The in-phase and quadrature samples enter as two packed buses, each containing \(G_{\mathrm{NUM\_SYM}}=32\) signed Q1.15 samples. From this common input, the datapath splits into three parallel branches:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item the \ac{SBPS} chain estimates the wrapped centre phase of the block,
    \item the \ac{SE} block estimates the signed phase spread over the block,
    \item an explicit I/Q delay line preserves the original input block until the phase estimates are available.
\end{enumerate}

This structure is important because the spread estimator in the implemented top design operates on the original received block, not on a block that has already been derotated by the \ac{SBPS} result. The \ac{SBPS} and \ac{SE} branches therefore both observe the same noisy, phase-impaired input block. Their outputs describe complementary aspects of that same block: \(\phi_e\) is the wrapped centre-phase estimate, while \(\theta\) is the signed phase-spread estimate.
An overview is given in the following figure:

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/top.png}
\caption{Top overview implemented algorithm}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Functional dataflow}

At the top-design boundary, \texttt{in\_valid} is distributed to both the \ac{SBPS} chain and the \ac{SE} block. The \ac{SBPS} chain processes the full block through its serial refinement stages and outputs the selected phase estimate as trigonometric coefficients. Internally these coefficients leave the \ac{SBPS} IP as Q1.31 cosine and sine values. Before they enter the \textit{PUPC} block, the \textit{trigQ31\_to\_Q15} adapter truncates them to Q1.15 by dropping the lower fractional bits (Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow-top}). This adapter is purely combinational, so it does not alter the valid timing of the \ac{SBPS} output.

In parallel, the \ac{SE} block outputs the signed spread estimate \(\theta\) directly as a 32-bit Q4.28 phase word. This representation matches the downstream phase-unwrapping and phase-compensation logic, so no numerical format conversion is needed at the top-design boundary. Only a timing-alignment delay is inserted before \(\theta\) is passed to \textit{PUPC}.

The third branch contains the raw I/Q block delay. This branch carries the original Q1.15 samples, still packed as separate I and Q buses. The delayed block is the block that \textit{PUPC} finally rotates. Consequently, the final compensation stage applies the centre phase and phase-spread information to the same sample block from which these estimates were derived.

After these top-level alignment operations, \textit{PUPC} performs the remaining phase-domain processing. First, the Q1.15 trigonometric \ac{SBPS} result is converted to a Q4.28 phase word using the atan2 path described in the phase-compensation section. The delayed \(\theta\) value and delayed I/Q block are aligned to this conversion latency inside \textit{PUPC}. The phase unwrapper then combines the wrapped centre phase and signed spread estimate to maintain the correct \(\pi/2\) branch across successive blocks. Finally, the compensation-coefficient generator expands the block-level phase description into section phases and then to per-symbol \ac{CORDIC} rotation phases.

\subsection{Timing and alignment}

The top design uses fixed-latency valid pulses rather than a back-pressure handshake. The \texttt{in\_valid} pulse launches the \ac{SBPS} and \ac{SE} branches at the same clock edge, and every following alignment decision is expressed as a deterministic delay. This is why the wrapper contains two explicit alignment blocks in addition to the algorithmic modules.

The first alignment block is the \textit{synchroniser}. It delays the signed Q4.28 spread estimate until it is temporally aligned with the \ac{SBPS} centre-phase estimate. Its delay is parameterised as

\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{sync}}
    =
    G_{\mathrm{SBPS\_STAGES}}G_{\mathrm{SBPS\_STAGEDELAY}}
    -
    G_{\mathrm{SE\_DELAY}} .
\end{equation}

The assertion inside this block checks that the configured \ac{SBPS} latency model is not shorter than the configured \ac{SE} latency. If both branches are already aligned, the block becomes a pass-through; otherwise, both the valid pulse and \(\theta\) data word are shifted through the same delay line. In the generated block design, the \ac{SBPS} valid pulse is used as the master valid into \textit{PUPC}, while the synchronised \(\theta\) data is routed to the \textit{PUPC} theta input. This makes the top-level timing contract explicit: \(\theta\) must be delayed until it is valid on the same cycle as the \ac{SBPS} output coefficients.

The second alignment block is the \textit{iq\_block\_delay}. It delays the full packed I/Q block by the same configured \ac{SBPS} latency model:

\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{IQ}}
    =
    G_{\mathrm{SBPS\_STAGES}}G_{\mathrm{SBPS\_STAGEDELAY}} .
\end{equation}

This delay is needed because the samples themselves do not pass through the \ac{SBPS} or \ac{SE} estimation pipelines before correction. Without the delay, \textit{PUPC} would receive a phase estimate belonging to an older block while rotating the newest input block. By delaying the raw block at the top level, the final rotation stage receives three mutually aligned quantities: the wrapped centre-phase estimate, the signed spread estimate and the corresponding received samples.

There is one additional alignment boundary inside \textit{PUPC}. The \ac{SBPS} phase enters \textit{PUPC} as Q1.15 sine and cosine, whereas the unwrapper and compensation logic operate on Q4.28 phase words. The atan2 conversion therefore defines a local timing point inside \textit{PUPC}; the spread estimate and the I/Q block are delayed by the same conversion latency before entering the phase-unwrapping and section-generation backend. This second alignment step is local to the compensation block, while the \textit{synchroniser} and \textit{iq\_block\_delay} solve the inter-block timing at the top-design level.

Functionally, the top design can therefore be viewed as a three-input merge into \textit{PUPC}: a wrapped centre phase from \ac{SBPS}, a signed phase spread from \ac{SE}, and the delayed original sample block. The additional wrapper blocks do not change the phase-recovery algorithm itself; they ensure that the different numerical representations and pipeline latencies meet at the same block boundary.
